Sixty years of matchmaking. Not one controlled trial.

Humans have been letting machines pick their partners since punch card dating in 1965. Dating companies have published research when it flattered them — but not one has ever run the controlled trial that could have proved them wrong. So we built an app to run it, and we need a hundred people to help.

Under all of them, not a single controlled trial of whether any of it works.

eHarmony published in PNAS. Tawkify published last year. Every one of those studies was observational — comparing people who chose to sign up against people who didn’t. Nobody has ever randomised anything.

Why everyone?

Because a shortlist tells you nothing. If we picked who you met, we could never know whether it worked — we would only be measuring our own guess. Meeting all fifty is the only way to find out who you actually respond to, and it is the only way anyone can tell the difference between a good match and a good mood.

Why three minutes?

It is long enough to know something and short enough to do fifty-two of them. And the research is consistent: the sooner two people meet, the better it goes. Weeks of messaging first makes things worse, not better, because you build an impression reality can’t match.

Why an algorithm nobody has tried?

Deferred acceptance won the Nobel in 2012 and runs the medical residency match. It is literally called the stable marriage problem. It has never once been used on dating, because it needs real, relatively-ranked preferences — and nobody has bothered to collect any. After fifty-two dates, you will have given us yours.

What do I get out of it?

A ranked list of fifty people you actually met, an introduction to the one the algorithm says fits, and a model of your own taste that will probably surprise you. Also: whatever we find, we publish — including if it turns out none of this works.

I built a dating app that led to thousands of marriages, but I can’t tell you how many, or what made those couples work and others fail. No one in our industry can.

The industry spends billions on features and almost nothing finding out whether any of them work. This is what I’d build if the goal were aligning incentives with users and moving the science forward, instead of growing a subscription base.

Amanda Bradford, founder
